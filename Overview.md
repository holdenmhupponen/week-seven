# Overview

This week, we learned about the application of network analyses in archaeological data. A network analysis broadly means an analysis that focuses on the connections between nodes rather than the nodes themselves. Some of the specific variables of such analysis include betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, and modularity; or, the measure of how often a node appears as the shortest path between two others, how close one node is to the other nodes, and how much a given network can be broken down into distinct communities or not. On centrality, Mills uses the example of early Japanese settlements with a higher centrality value as having more influence than those with a lower value throughout its history. She also discusses the concept of "weak ties": As I understand it, this is a positive factor of network analyses wherein a node that sits between two otherwise unrelated nodes is less relegated to its community in terms of the information it has access to. So, in other words, a higher betweenness centrality suggests a greater amount of external information in that node. Though I am not sure how I would test this, this theory should, if my understanding is correct, mean that Rhodos and Lilybaeum, which had the highest between centrality values, should have a disproportionately higher access to information than the other settlements in that dataset. I think here is where the limitations discussed by Brughman are evident, though. In his paper, he uses a case study of applying a network analysis to Roman table wares. It broadly analyzed whether the presence of table wares in a node (settlement/city) was proportional to a short distance in between nodes and a node's distance to table ware production facilities. For some cases, this proved to be a valuable tool, as in the case of "Eastern Sigillata A distribution on Levantine sites." Yet this analysis fell short in explaining the wares in nodes like "Corinth, Knossos and Alexandria" that have a greater presence of wares than their distances between other nodes and to table ware production facilities might suggest. That is, the network analysis did not take into account the political and historical contexts of these nodes. Brughman uses this oversight to suggest that the field of archaeology needs to develop its own archaeological network analysis. Relying on non-specialized network analysis frameworks means flattening the unique analyses of the archaeological field in order to fit into the frameworks of other disciplines. In my experience with the Roman settlements Google Colab, I can see how these limitations may have played out. This may include a flawed overstatement of certain settlements' importance in the history of Rome. However, I think that the Google Colab exercise was deliberately built with these limitations in mind; that is, it is like a working example of Brughman's call for a specialized archaeological network analysis. The observation sections had us inquire about the data not in a networked vacuum, but as it related to the political and historical factors at play.
